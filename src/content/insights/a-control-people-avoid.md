---
title: A Control People Avoid Is Not a Control
label: Insight
relatedChapters: ["settling-the-few-things", "ownership"]
summary: A gate that costs more than the decision is worth does not get failed. It gets routed around, and it sees less than it did before it existed.
---

A control only controls the decisions that actually reach it.

That sounds obvious written down, and it is routinely missed in practice, because the people designing a control are imagining the decisions they want to see rather than the effort required to bring one. If getting on the agenda takes two weeks, preparation takes half a day, and surviving the discussion takes a certain appetite for being questioned by people with less context — then the control has quietly set a price. Any decision worth less than that price will not be brought.

What follows is not defiance. It is arithmetic, performed by reasonable people. Some decisions get made quietly and never surface. Others do not get made at all, and sit waiting for someone with the energy to go through the process. Both outcomes are worse than the state the control was created to improve, and the second is worse than the first.

So the gate ends up seeing a biased sample: the large decisions, the political ones, and the ones whose owners had time. It sees fewer of the small compounding choices that actually shape a system, which are precisely the ones a second pair of eyes might have helped.

A control that people avoid is not a control. It is a tollbooth on a road with a bypass, and everyone has found the bypass.

The test is cheap and slightly uncomfortable: ask what has *not* come to your forum in the last quarter, and why. If the honest answer is that it wasn't worth the trip, the forum is no longer measuring risk. It is measuring stamina.
